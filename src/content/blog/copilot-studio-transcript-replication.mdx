Copilot Studio records what people said to your agents. It writes those
conversations into a Dataverse table called `conversationtranscript`, as a raw
JSON activity log, and then a recurring bulk-delete job removes anything older
than 30 days.

So if you want to report on agent adoption over a year, you have a replication
problem before you have a reporting problem. This post covers the three ways to
solve it, what each one costs you, and the single question that usually settles
it.

## The short version

At low volume with no need for near-real-time data, almost every criterion that
normally separates these options stops mattering. Latency stops mattering.
Throughput stops mattering. Dataverse service protection limits stop mattering.
Storage cost becomes a rounding error everywhere.

What is left is one financial question and one durable operational one.

/>

**If you already own a Fabric capacity:** use Link to Microsoft Fabric with
low-latency sync. It is about an hour of clicking, costs effectively nothing
incremental, and Microsoft operates it.

**If you do not, and would be buying one purely to report on this table:** a
timer-triggered Azure Function is meaningfully cheaper — on the order of $2,000
to $10,000 a year — and AI-assisted development has turned the build from a
project into a scripted deployment.

The remaining trade-off is not cost or speed. It is who owns the pipeline, and
who notices when it silently stops.

## What makes this table different, in every option

Several characteristics of `conversationtranscript` survive the low-volume
assumption entirely, and they apply no matter which transport you pick.

**The `Content` column is a raw JSON activity log.** It holds the full Bot
Framework activity stream: message, event and trace activities, channel data,
variable assignments, customer satisfaction ("CSAT") responses, intent
recognition. None of the three
options parse this for you. Whichever transport you choose, you still need a
shredding layer before Power BI is usable — and that is the largest single piece
of work in the project. It is identical in all three options, which is why it
never appears as a differentiator and always appears in the schedule.

**There is a 1 MB cap per record.** Larger conversations are split into multiple
rows sharing the same `Name` and `ConversationStartTime`, distinguished by
`Metadata.BatchId`. [Microsoft documents the reassembly
rule](https://learn.microsoft.com/microsoft-copilot-studio/analytics-transcripts-powerapps):
take all records with the same `Name` and `ConversationStartTime`, then sort by
`BatchId`. Get it wrong and your longest conversations — the ones actually worth
analyzing — silently truncate.

**A bulk-delete job purges rows older than 30 days.** This is the single biggest
trap, and low volume does not soften it. The job is called *Bulk Delete
Conversation Transcript Records Older Than 1 Month*, and you can cancel it and
create a replacement with a longer window. Until you do, a replica is not an
archive: a delete in Dataverse propagates downstream and takes your reporting
history with it.

**Transcript content is sensitive.** Transcripts can carry user-entered personal
data, hashed user IDs, variable values and knowledge-source content. Dataverse
security roles — including the **Bot Transcript Viewer** role — do not follow the
data into Fabric. Downstream access is governed by Fabric workspace and OneLake
permissions instead, so the list of people who can read transcripts changes the
moment you replicate them.

**Rows arrive in bursts.** A transcript row is written after about 30 minutes of
conversation inactivity. Even at low total volume the arrival pattern is lumpy,
which is a further argument against caring about instantaneous freshness.

**Some agents produce nothing at all.** Microsoft documents that transcripts are
not written for Microsoft Dataverse for Teams environments or Microsoft 365
Copilot agents. Confirm your agents actually produce rows before you build
anything — and measure per environment rather than assuming which types are
excluded, because filtering by environment type is a one-line configuration
change that can
silently drop data with nothing in the pipeline reporting an error.

## What low volume actually neutralizes

This is the part that changes the shape of the decision, so it is worth being
explicit.

| Normally a differentiator     | Why it stops mattering here                                                                                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sync latency                  | Low-latency sync delivers a roughly two-minute median against about an hour for classic. If the business reads a weekly report, all three are equivalent. |
| Throughput ceilings           | Published ceilings are orders of magnitude above what this workload generates.                                                                            |
| Service protection limits     | A paged delta read of a few thousand rows uses a handful of requests per run. The only real exposure is the one-time backfill.                             |
| Storage cost                  | 2,000 conversations a day at 30 KB is about 22 GB a year. Cents per month, everywhere.                                                                     |
| Build effort                  | AI-generated code plus infrastructure as code makes the Function a scripted deployment rather than a project.                                              |

And what it promotes:

| Now decisive                     | Why                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Do you already own a capacity?   | Link to Fabric delivers into OneLake, and consuming that requires a Fabric capacity. If you have one, marginal cost is ~$0. If not, you are buying a platform to report on 22 GB a year. |
| Who owns and monitors it?        | With cost and speed neutralized, operational ownership is the last real difference — and the Function's worst failure mode is a timer that quietly stops firing.                        |
| Retention beyond 30 days         | Low volume makes long retention cheap and therefore attractive. But replicas propagate deletes, so this has to be answered deliberately in every option.                                  |
| Data sensitivity                 | Unchanged by volume. If transcripts must never land unredacted, only the Function can transform before persisting.                                                                       |

## Option 1 — Link to Fabric, classic engine

Link to Fabric is built into Power Apps. You select an environment and the tables
you want, Dataverse replicates them into a Microsoft-managed lake, and Fabric
surfaces them in a Lakehouse through OneLake shortcuts. The classic engine reads
from the Dataverse database, serializes to an intermediate CSV, then converts to
Delta Parquet. You also get a SQL analytics endpoint and a default semantic model.

**Pros.** Zero code and zero infrastructure. No pipeline to babysit and no
secrets to rotate. It does not consume your Dataverse API entitlement. Microsoft
operates it, with a real support path. Its slower latency is a non-issue for this
workload.

**Cons.** It has been superseded — low-latency sync is the same product with a
better engine and is now what new links get. The three-hop path means more
failure points and slower recovery. No transformation, so the JSON lands as-is.
Deletes replicate. Dataverse security roles do not carry into Fabric. Column type
or precision changes cause permanent sync failure, and the fix is to remove and
re-add the table.

**Verdict:** not something to choose deliberately in 2026. If your station has
low-latency sync, use that. If it does not, classic is a perfectly adequate
placeholder precisely because you do not need the speed.

## Option 2 — Link to Fabric with low-latency sync

Low-latency sync removes the intermediate CSV step entirely: data flows from the
Dataverse database directly to Delta Parquet. That one architectural change
delivers both the speed and the reliability benefits.

There is **no opt-in**. The engine rolls out station by station, and once your
station is enabled, every new link uses it automatically. You can confirm it by
looking for the **Low-latency mode** flag on the link in the **Link data** list.
Existing links stay on the previous engine until you unlink and relink, which
triggers a full initial sync — at this volume, a minor event rather than a
weekend job.

The benefit that actually matters here is not speed. It is that removing a hop
eliminates a whole class of transient I/O, encoding and throttling errors, and
shortens recovery. Reliability, not latency, is what you are buying.

Leave **Change Data Feed** off. It gives you roughly 24 hours of row-level change
events, and Microsoft notes it is memory- and compute-intensive and can increase
overall latency. It solves a problem this workload does not have.

**Pros.** The lowest total effort of the three: an hour of clicking, then
Microsoft runs it forever. Effectively zero incremental cost if a capacity
already exists. More reliable than classic. Consumes no Dataverse API
entitlement. Strategically aligned, since it is the default for new links.

**Cons.** It requires a Fabric capacity to consume — and that is the whole
decision if you do not have one. Availability is station-dependent. No
transformation, so personal data cannot be redacted before it lands. Deletes
still propagate. Row-level security still does not carry through. Timestamps are
written as INT64 rather than INT96, which breaks downstream readers that assume
otherwise. Published latency figures are indicative telemetry, explicitly not a
service-level agreement ("SLA").

**Verdict:** the default choice, provided a capacity already exists. If it does
not, this option carries a four-figure annual platform cost for a workload
producing a couple of gigabytes a year.

## Option 3 — Azure Functions on a timer

This is the option worth explaining properly, because the one-paragraph version
of it is misleading in several specific ways. What follows comes from a working
tenant-wide reference implementation that I built, tore down, and rebuilt end to
end — so the traps below are ones that actually fired, not ones I anticipated on
paper.

The headline claim survives: a competent timer-triggered sync is now a scripted
deployment rather than a project. Several things it implies do not.

### The shape of it

<Diagram
  svg={architecture}
  caption="Four stages. The ordering in stage four is the part people get wrong."
/>

A timer-triggered Function runs every 30 minutes. It authenticates to Dataverse
using a user-assigned managed identity federated onto a secretless Entra app
registration, whose client ID is registered as a Dataverse application user. It
keeps a `createdon` high-water mark per environment, reassembles multi-part
records, optionally redacts, then appends to the destination.

Two details in that diagram are load-bearing:

**30 minutes is not arbitrary.** Dataverse writes a transcript only after 30
minutes of conversation inactivity, so a tighter schedule adds request pressure
without adding freshness. Also worth knowing: the timer binding reads its
schedule when the host starts, so changing it needs a restart — and you should
verify the next fire time rather than assuming.

**The HTTP trigger is not optional in practice.** It is how you run the initial
backfill, prove a fix, and recover a missed window without waiting for the next
tick. Leaving it out is a decision you will regret the first time something goes
wrong at 4pm on a Friday.

A useful property falls out of the tenant-wide shape: because discovery is
tenant-wide and application users are provisioned on the fly, one deployment
covers every environment and picks up newly created ones without manual work.
Link to Fabric is configured per environment, so a tenant with a long tail of
environments is a genuine point in the Function's favor. It is also a reason to
be careful, for reasons in the privilege section below.

### The identity chain is three parties, not one

"Managed identity, so no secrets" is true, and it is not the whole story.
Reaching Dataverse app-only takes a three-legged exchange, and each leg has a
failure mode that does not announce itself.

<Diagram
  svg={identityChain}
  caption="Each leg fails differently, and two of the three fail silently."
/>

The managed identity **cannot** be the Dataverse application user. Power Platform
binds application users to an Entra app registration's client ID, and tenant-wide
environment listing only works for an app registered as a Power Platform
management application. A managed identity is neither of those things.

Two of these cost me real time:

- The federated credential's subject must be the managed identity's **object
  (principal) ID**, not its client ID. Entra accepts the wrong value without
  complaint, and the failure surfaces much later as a token exchange that fails
  with nothing useful attached.
- The environment-listing scope contains a double slash that looks like a typo
  and is not. "Fixing" it breaks the token request.

**One step cannot be automated.** A service principal cannot register itself as a
Power Platform management application, by design. Whatever else you script, one
interactive administrator sign-in is required the first time. The bootstrap is
also circular: the infrastructure needs the app registration's client ID, and the
federated credential needs the managed identity's principal ID, which only exists
after the infrastructure is deployed. That is scriptable, but it is not one
template you hand to a pipeline and forget.

### Change tracking is the wrong incremental mechanism here

Dataverse change tracking with delta links is the obvious mechanism, and it is
the wrong one for this table. This is worth stating plainly because it is the
design decision most likely to be made by reflex — including by an AI assistant
generating the code, which will reach for it confidently.

- **Delta links propagate deletes.** The 30-day bulk-delete job would erase the
  archive the pipeline exists to build. A delete-aware sync is precisely what you
  do not want.
- **`$filter`, `$orderby`, `$top` and `$expand` are rejected** when the change
  tracking header is present. No way to bound a run, order it deterministically,
  or resume after a failure.
- **Delta tokens expire**, defaulting to seven days. An outage longer than that
  forces a full reseed — and at that point there is nothing older than 30 days
  left in Dataverse to reseed from.

What works instead: a per-environment high-water mark on `createdon`, a
deliberate overlap window replayed on every run, and deduplication at the
destination keyed on the transcript ID. The overlap exists because transcripts
are written only after a period of inactivity and can land slightly out of order
relative to a strictly advancing watermark. Duplicates are produced on purpose
and removed downstream. A second run that returns a few rows rather than zero is
correct behavior, not a bug.

The same delete-propagation trap applies to Link to Fabric and to Azure Synapse
Link. This is not really an argument for the Function so much as a reminder that
the retention question has to be answered explicitly in whichever option you
pick.

### The shredding traps that fail silently

Microsoft documents the activity fields well. What is less obvious is the
top-level shape of the payload, and every trap below fails quietly — no
exception, no parse error, just wrong numbers in a report someone will act on.

| Trap                                   | What goes wrong                                                                                                                                                                 |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `Content` has more than one encoding   | I found three: a bare array of activities, an object with an `activities` property, and a double-encoded object whose `text` property is itself a JSON string. Handling only one yields zero activities, which reads as an empty conversation rather than a bug. |
| The speaker role is numeric            | `from.role` is `0` for the agent and `1` for the user. This *is* documented — but `role` is a string elsewhere in the Bot Framework schema, so it is easy to invert. Getting it backwards does not fail; it silently attributes every agent utterance to the customer. |
| Timestamps have more than one format   | Documented as epoch seconds, and that is what I mostly saw — but epoch milliseconds and ISO 8601 also occur. Turns land in 1970 or thousands of years in the future.              |
| The 1 MB cap splits conversations      | Order by `BatchId` before pairing prompts with responses, or your longest conversations truncate.                                                                                |
| Two bot identifiers exist              | The agent ID in `Metadata` and the Dataverse `bot` table key are different ID spaces. Only the second joins to the `bot` table; joining on the wrong one returns nothing at all. The agent name in `Metadata` is the schema name, not the display name. |
| `Name` is a composite                  | It is the conversation ID, an underscore, then the bot ID — and a conversation ID can itself contain underscores. Splitting on the separator corrupts it; trim the known suffix instead. |
| User identity is hashed                | `from.id` is hashed and cannot be resolved to a person. Authenticated channels also carry the real Entra object ID, and only that one is resolvable.                              |

One more, which is documented but easy to miss and changes what your reporting
means: when SharePoint is used as a knowledge source, the agent's *answer* is not
included in the transcript. It is marked `REDACTED`. You get the question and the
source content, not the response.

And one that reframes every adoption number you produce: `ConversationInfo`
carries an `isDesignMode` flag marking test-pane traffic — a maker testing their
own agent. In my build, **28 of 33 sessions were design mode**. If you do not
filter it out, you are reporting on your own team.

### The privilege problem

"Least privilege on the application user" is achievable, but not on the path that
makes this option attractive.

The endpoint that provisions Dataverse application users across environments
always grants **System Administrator**, and offers no way to choose a role. That
behavior is exactly what makes automatic, self-healing coverage of new
environments possible. It is also far more privilege than reading two tables
requires, in every environment in the tenant.

The mitigation is real and it costs you the automation: turn auto-provisioning
off, ship a custom security role granting Read at organization scope on the agent
and transcript tables in a small solution, and assign application users yourself
per environment. You trade self-healing discovery for least privilege, and you
own keeping the role deployed as environments are created.

Worth raising in design rather than in review: Link to Fabric needs no
tenant-wide application user at all. If your security review is going to object
to a broadly privileged application identity across every environment, that
objection lands on this option alone.

### The destination decides the bill

This is the part most cost comparisons get wrong, including the one I started
from.

<Diagram
  svg={destinationCost}
  caption="The code is nearly free. The destination is not."
/>

My reference build landed in Azure Data Explorer, which is genuinely the nicest
technical fit for this data: native dynamic JSON parsing in Kusto Query Language
("KQL"), update policies
to shred activities on ingest, per-table retention, materialized views for
deduplication. It is also the version that quietly costs the most, because even
the cheapest development tier is a standing hourly charge for a single instance
with no SLA — and that charge dwarfs a couple of gigabytes a year.

Two further items are routinely left out of Function estimates:

- **Private endpoints are billed per endpoint.** Hardening the storage account
  typically means blob, queue and table endpoints, each with its own hourly
  charge plus data processing. That is material against a $10 baseline. Worth
  noting that the Flex Consumption plan supports virtual network integration
  natively, so networking no longer forces you onto a Premium plan — the
  endpoints are the cost, not the plan.
- **Observability is small but not zero**, and it is the last component you
  should economize on. The dominant risk at low volume is a timer that quietly
  stops firing, which produces no exception for logging to catch — so alert on
  the *absence* of new rows rather than on errors, and track coverage per
  environment rather than a single tenant-wide count.

If cost is the reason you are considering this option at all: land the raw JSON
in Blob or Azure Data Lake Storage ("ADLS"), build the shredded turn-level table
there, and serve a Power BI
Pro scheduled import. Choose a Kusto-style destination when the KQL shredding,
retention policies and materialized views are worth paying for on their own
merits — not as a default.

### Effort, stated honestly

AI-assisted development genuinely collapses the build. It is worth being precise
about what it does and does not cover, because the gap is where projects overrun.

**Covered:** Function code — authentication, paging, throttling, watermarking,
retries. Infrastructure as code and deployment scripts. Destination schema and
ingestion mapping. A first pass at shredding and pairing logic. Draft
documentation.

**Not covered:** The interactive administrator step. Validating the undocumented
content shapes against *your* transcripts. Deciding the privilege trade-off and
living with it. Monitoring, alerting, a runbook, disaster recovery and change
control. Knowing, a year later, why any of it works.

A fair summary: about half an hour to a running pipeline, and about a day to a
pipeline you would be willing to leave unattended for a year. The second number
is the one that belongs in the comparison — and it is still small, which is
exactly why the deciding factor is ownership rather than effort.

## A fourth option worth knowing about

Low volume and relaxed freshness make one more approach viable that would be
indefensible at scale: a Power BI dataflow or semantic model connecting directly
to Dataverse on a scheduled refresh, with no replication layer at all. No Azure
resources, no Fabric capacity, no code.

Know the trade-offs before taking it. Power Query is a poor tool for parsing
large JSON blobs and will be slow and brittle against a 1 MB `Content` column.
Refreshes consume Dataverse API entitlement. Power BI Pro is limited to eight
scheduled refreshes a day. And it does nothing to preserve history unless you
configure incremental refresh carefully, so the 30-day purge remains live. Treat
it as a prototyping route or a genuinely minimal need, not as a platform.

## Cost, at a realistic volume

Assume 2,000 conversations a day at 30 KB average — roughly 1.8 GB a month, 22 GB
a year of raw JSON. Every option handles that without breathing hard.

| Scenario                          | Approach                                        | Indicative annual cost                                    |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Capacity already owned            | Link to Fabric with low-latency sync            | ~$0 incremental, plus a few dollars of OneLake storage     |
| No capacity, buy one for this     | Purchase F2 or larger purely to enable the link | Roughly $3,150 a year pay-as-you-go for F2, more for a usable reporting size |
| No capacity, build instead        | Function on a timer into Blob/ADLS or Azure SQL, Power BI Pro import | Under $120 a year in cloud spend                          |

The F2 figure is arithmetic rather than a quote: Fabric capacity bills at $0.18
per capacity-unit-hour pay-as-you-go, F2 is 2 CUs, and 2 × $0.18 × 730 hours is
$262.80 a month. Reserved pricing is materially lower. Check the [Azure pricing
calculator](https://azure.microsoft.com/pricing/calculator/) and your own
agreement before budgeting — these are list-price estimates for relative
comparison, not quotes.

The gap between the second and third rows is the whole argument. Everything else
in this post is a tiebreaker.

## Before you decide

Confirm these in the target environment. Several have changed the answer on real
projects.

1. **Do you already own a Fabric capacity, and at what size?** This is the primary
   determinant. Everything else is a tiebreaker.
2. **Are transcripts actually being written?** Confirm rows exist and recording
   is not disabled by policy — and measure per environment rather than assuming
   which types are excluded.
3. **What is the actual daily row count and average `Content` size?** This
   confirms the low-volume assumption everything above rests on.
4. **How stale can the report be before anyone complains?** Get a number. If the
   honest answer is minutes, the analysis changes.
5. **What is the current bulk-delete retention, and what does the business need?**
   This decides whether you need an append-only archive at all — and it is the
   decision most projects get wrong by deferring it.
6. **How many transcripts exceed 1 MB and split across batches?** Tells you how
   much reassembly logic you need, and how wrong naive reporting would be.
7. **Do transcripts contain regulated personal data?** If yes, this may force the
   Function regardless of capacity.
8. **Who is allowed to see transcript content, and does Fabric workspace access
   match that list?** The Bot Transcript Viewer role does not follow the data.
9. **Has your station received low-latency sync?** Look for the Low-latency mode
   flag.
10. **Who owns this pipeline in three years, and who is on call when it silently
    stops firing?** Build effort is now trivial. Ownership is the real question,
    and this is the one that settles the debate.

## Implementation order, either path

Whichever option you land on, do these in this order:

1. **Decide retention at the source first.** If you need more than 30 days,
   cancel the default bulk-delete job and create a replacement, or plan an
   append-only archive. Before anything else.
2. **Stand up the transport.** Enable the link and confirm the Low-latency mode
   flag, or deploy the Function and register its identity.
3. **Land raw first.** Keep an untouched copy of the JSON in cheap storage. It
   costs almost nothing and it saves you when the shredding logic turns out to be
   wrong — which it will, at least once.
4. **Shred the JSON.** Reassemble multi-part records, then explode the activity
   array into a turn-level fact table with outcome, CSAT, topic, channel and turn
   counts.
5. **Model for Power BI.** Serve conversation-level and turn-level aggregates.
   Never point Power BI at raw JSON.
6. **Add a freshness alert.** Alert on the absence of new rows rather than on
   errors. This is the failure mode that actually happens.
7. **Restrict access deliberately.** Dataverse roles do not follow the data.

## The one-line version

If you have a Fabric capacity, use low-latency sync and spend your time on the
shredding layer instead. If you do not, build the Function — but budget for the
day of hardening rather than the half hour of scaffolding, and put the freshness
alert in before you put it into production.
